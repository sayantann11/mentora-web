Parents today have more options than ever for supporting their children: tuition classes, online tutors, school counsellors, child psychologists, career counsellors, coaches and mentors. The names overlap, and it isn’t always obvious which one your child actually needs.

This guide explains each role in plain language — what they do, what they don’t, and how to decide.

## The short version

| | Focus | Typical format | Good for | Not for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tutor** | School subjects | Group or 1-on-1 classes | Understanding topics, exam practice | Emotional or life-skills support |
| **Mentor** | Life skills and personal growth | Regular 1-on-1 conversations | Confidence, habits, decisions, direction | Diagnosis or treatment |
| **School counsellor** | Wellbeing within school | Sessions at school | Adjustment, conflicts, early concerns | Long-term clinical treatment |
| **Psychologist / psychiatrist** | Mental health | Clinical sessions | Diagnosable conditions, high-risk situations | Everyday coaching |
| **Career counsellor** | Course and career choices | Few focused sessions, often with tests | Specific stream or college decisions | Ongoing personal support |

## Tutors: learning the subject

A tutor’s job is academic: explaining concepts, practising problems, preparing for tests. A good tutor can also build confidence in a subject. But tutoring is focused on *what* a child learns, not on how they feel, why they procrastinate or what they want from life.

**Choose a tutor when** your child understands the importance of studying and is trying, but struggles with the content itself.

## Mentors: growing as a person

A mentor is a trusted adult who meets a child regularly to help them grow — in confidence, communication, habits, emotional awareness, decision-making and direction. Mentoring is non-clinical: a mentor doesn’t diagnose or treat anything. Their tools are listening, asking good questions and helping the child build their own solutions.

The defining feature of mentoring is the **relationship over time**. A mentor remembers last week’s conversation, follows up on the small goal the child chose and notices patterns.

**Choose a mentor when** your child is broadly okay but could use support with everyday challenges — exam stress, peer pressure, screen habits, low confidence, or figuring out what they want — and would benefit from someone outside the family to talk to.

## Counsellors: support for difficulties

Counsellors are trained to help people work through emotional, behavioural, academic or social difficulties. Many schools have a counsellor; there are also counsellors in private practice. Counselling may involve structured techniques and is generally more focused on specific difficulties than mentoring.

**Choose a counsellor when** your child is struggling in a way that is affecting daily life — ongoing sadness, anxiety, school refusal, family conflict, grief — and you want trained, focused support.

## Psychologists and psychiatrists: professional mental-health care

Clinical psychologists assess and treat mental-health conditions through therapy. Psychiatrists are medical doctors who can diagnose conditions and prescribe medication. These are the right professionals for clinical concerns or situations that involve risk.

**Seek professional mental-health care when** you notice signs such as persistent hopelessness, self-harm, talk of not wanting to live, severe anxiety or panic, eating problems, or sudden major changes in behaviour. **In an emergency, call 112.** For free 24×7 support and guidance, **Tele-MANAS: 14416 or 1-800-891-4416**.

## Career counsellors: specific decisions

Career counsellors focus on education and career choices. They often use aptitude or interest tests and help students compare streams, courses and colleges. This is usually a short engagement around a decision point, such as after Class 10.

**Choose a career counsellor when** your child faces a specific decision and needs information about options and requirements.

## How these roles work together

These options aren’t competitors. Many children benefit from more than one at different times. For example, a Class 11 student might have a physics tutor, a mentor to help with discipline and stress, and a short engagement with a career counsellor before applications.

What matters most is **clear boundaries**. A tutor shouldn’t be playing therapist. A mentor shouldn’t be handling a clinical concern. Responsible services know their limits and refer onwards when needed.

## Questions to ask any provider

Whoever you choose, it’s fair to ask:

- What exactly will my child do in a session?
- What are your qualifications and training?
- How do you handle confidentiality, and when would you tell me something?
- What happens if you notice a serious concern?
- What will I hear about progress, and how often?
- Are there rules about contacting my child outside sessions?

A trustworthy provider will answer these clearly and in writing.
