Somewhere between Class 6 and Class 8, the phone stops being a thing your child borrows and becomes a thing your child lives inside. Homework happens on it. Friendships happen on it. School groups, games, videos and music all happen on it. And for many families, so do the arguments.

If you’re struggling with your pre-teen’s screen time, this article won’t tell you to throw the phone away. It will explain why bans tend to backfire at this age, what the phone is often doing for your child, and how to build screen habits they actually keep.

## Why bans backfire for pre-teens

Pre-teens are wired to push for independence. That’s healthy — it’s how they eventually become capable adults. But it means a rule imposed from above (“No phone after 7, end of discussion”) often becomes a battle of wills. The phone gets used secretly, the rule gets broken, trust drops on both sides, and the next rule is stricter.

Total bans also don’t teach anything. A child who has never practised putting the phone down on their own doesn’t magically learn it at 16, when you’re no longer in a position to enforce rules.

What works better is helping children understand their own habits, and agreeing on rules *with* them.

## What the phone is helping them avoid

Here’s a question we ask students at Mentora: “When you pick up your phone without meaning to, what were you doing just before?”

Usually, the answer is something uncomfortable:

- a hard maths problem;
- a boring chapter;
- feeling left out after seeing a group chat;
- being anxious about tomorrow’s test;
- being bored and not knowing what else to do.

Scrolling is a very effective way to stop feeling something unpleasant. That’s why willpower alone rarely works — the phone is solving a real problem, just not in a helpful way. Once a child can name what they’re escaping from, they can start finding better ways to handle it.

We use a simple model: **Situation → Thought → Feeling → Behaviour → Consequence.** For example: *homework is hard → “I can’t do this” → frustration → opens Instagram → homework isn’t done at 10 p.m.* Changing the thought (“I’ll do just the first two questions”) or the situation (phone in another room) breaks the chain.

## Signs screen time has become a problem

Every family’s limits are different. These are signs it might be time to act:

- sleep is regularly cut short by late-night use;
- homework is consistently unfinished or rushed;
- your child is irritable or panicky when separated from the device;
- they’ve dropped activities or friends they used to enjoy;
- their mood seems closely tied to likes, messages or game results.

## Co-creating a screen plan: a step-by-step approach

### Step 1: Start with curiosity, not accusation

Pick a calm moment — not right after an argument. Try: “I’ve noticed screen time is causing a lot of fights between us. I don’t want to keep fighting. Can we figure out a plan together?”

### Step 2: Let them describe their own week

Ask your child to look at their screen-time report with you, or estimate it honestly. Ask what they enjoy, what feels like a waste, and when they find it hardest to stop. Many pre-teens are surprised by their own numbers.

### Step 3: Agree on a few clear zones

Rather than an hour limit that’s hard to track, agree on **phone-free zones and times**. For example:

- no phones at meals;
- phones charge outside the bedroom overnight;
- one 30–45 minute phone-free homework block each weekday.

Start small. One phone-free homework block that actually happens is worth more than a perfect plan that doesn’t.

### Step 4: Make the alternative easier

If the phone is the easiest thing to reach for, it will win. Make the alternative easy too: a book on the bed, a sketchbook on the table, a ball near the door, a friend to call. Help your child plan *what they will do instead*.

### Step 5: Review it weekly

Check in once a week: what worked, what didn’t, what should change? Adjust together. This review is where children learn to manage themselves.

### Step 6: Model it

Children notice when parents scroll at dinner. If the family agrees on phone-free meals, that includes adults.

## What to do when the plan breaks

It will break. That’s normal. Treat it as information: “The plan didn’t work on Tuesday. What happened?” Maybe Tuesday is tuition night and the homework block clashes. Maybe a friend was upset and messaging. Adjust the plan rather than adding punishments.

## A note on online safety

Alongside habits, pre-teens need to know about online safety: not sharing personal information, recognising when someone online makes them uncomfortable, and knowing they can tell you without losing their phone. That last part matters — children hide problems when they fear the device will be confiscated.
