“How was school?”

“Fine.”

“What did you do today?”

“Nothing.”

If that exchange sounds familiar, you are in very good company. Almost every parent of a school-age child has had some version of it. The good news: a child who doesn’t answer a question isn’t necessarily a child who doesn’t want to talk. More often, it’s the question, the timing or the setting that isn’t working.

Here are seven changes that make children of every age — from Class 3 to Class 12 — more likely to open up.

## 1. Stop asking questions that have a one-word answer

“How was school?” is enormous. A whole day has to be summarised, judged and reported on, often while the child is tired and hungry. “Fine” is the most efficient answer.

Smaller, more specific questions are easier to answer:

- “What was the funniest thing that happened today?”
- “Who did you sit with at lunch?”
- “Was there a moment today when you felt bored?”
- “What’s one thing your teacher said that you didn’t agree with?”

For younger children, try games: “Tell me one true thing and one made-up thing about your day, and I’ll guess which is which.”

## 2. Talk side by side, not face to face

Many children — and especially teenagers — find eye contact during a serious conversation intense. Some of the best conversations happen when you’re doing something else together: in the car, cooking, walking, folding clothes, drawing. Being side by side lowers the pressure.

If your child tends to open up at bedtime or in the car, that’s not a coincidence. Protect those moments, and keep phones away from them.

## 3. Listen to the small things, so they bring you the big things

Children test the water. They tell you about a silly argument over a pencil, or a TV show, or a video game, and they watch how you respond. If those small things are met with distraction or a lecture, the big things — being bullied, feeling left out, being scared about exams — are less likely to follow.

Giving your full attention to the trivial stuff is how you earn the important stuff.

## 4. Hold back the lecture (even when it’s deserved)

When a child finally shares something difficult — “I didn’t do my homework because I was on my phone” — the instinct is to correct it immediately. But if every confession is followed by a lecture, children learn to stop confessing.

Try reflecting first: “Sounds like the phone was hard to put down tonight.” Then ask: “What do you think would help tomorrow?” You can still set boundaries. The order matters — understand first, guide second.

## 5. Name the feeling, not just the facts

Children often don’t have words for what they feel. When your child says “Riya didn’t invite me to her party,” you could respond with facts (“Maybe she forgot”) or with the feeling (“That must have felt really left out”).

Naming the feeling tells a child they’ve been understood. It also helps them build the vocabulary to describe their own emotions — one of the most important skills we work on at Mentora, under the theme of [emotional intelligence](/curriculum).

## 6. Share a little of yourself

Children open up more when the conversation isn’t one-way. Share a small, honest piece of your own day: “I was nervous before my presentation today.” “I had a disagreement with a colleague and I’m still thinking about it.”

You’re showing them that feelings are normal, that adults have hard days too, and that talking about it is something your family does.

## 7. Accept that you won’t always be the person they talk to

This is the hardest one. As children grow — especially from around Class 6 onwards — it’s healthy for them to start building trusted relationships outside the family: a cousin, a teacher, a coach, a mentor.

That isn’t a rejection of you. Developmentally, pre-teens and teenagers are learning to become independent people, and part of that is having some private space. What matters is that they have *someone* safe to talk to, and that you can see they’re doing okay.

## Age-specific tips

**Classes 3–5.** Use play, drawing and stories. Ask about characters in a book: “Why do you think she felt sad?” Children often talk about their own feelings through someone else’s.

**Classes 6–8.** Respect the new need for privacy. Knock before entering. Avoid teasing about friendships or crushes. Short, low-key check-ins work better than long talks.

**Classes 9–12.** Treat them as the young adult they’re becoming. Ask for their opinion on real decisions. Don’t take a closed door personally — keep the invitation open: “I’m around if you want to talk later.”

## When silence is a warning sign

A quiet child is not a problem in itself. But please take it seriously if your child suddenly withdraws from friends and activities they used to enjoy, stops eating or sleeping normally, talks about feeling hopeless, or shows any sign of self-harm. **In an emergency, call 112.** For free 24×7 mental-health support, call **Tele-MANAS on 14416 or 1-800-891-4416**, or speak to your doctor or school counsellor.
