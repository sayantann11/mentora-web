Board exams arrive with a particular kind of weight. For many Indian families, Class 10 and Class 12 results feel like they decide everything that follows — the stream, the college, the career, even how relatives will talk about your child at the next wedding. Teenagers feel that weight long before the timetable comes out.

Some stress is useful. It gets a student to open the book. But when stress becomes constant, it stops helping and starts getting in the way. This guide is about telling the difference, and about what parents can do at home that genuinely helps.

## What exam stress actually looks like

Stressed teenagers rarely say “I’m stressed.” More often you’ll notice:

- **Avoidance.** Long hours “studying” with very little done, or sudden interest in cleaning their room the night before a test.
- **Irritability.** Snapping at small questions, especially about marks or study plans.
- **Sleep changes.** Staying up very late, or sleeping much more than usual.
- **All-or-nothing talk.** “I’m going to fail anyway.” “There’s no point.” “Everyone else has finished the syllabus.”
- **Physical complaints.** Headaches, stomach aches or tiredness that cluster around tests.

None of these on their own means something is wrong. A pattern of several, lasting for weeks, is worth gently paying attention to.

## The thought behind the behaviour

At Mentora we use a simple model with students: **Situation → Thought → Feeling → Behaviour → Consequence.** Here’s how it often plays out before exams:

| Situation | Thought | Feeling | Behaviour | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-boards in three weeks | “I’ll fail” | Anxiety | Avoids opening the book | Falls further behind |

The behaviour (avoiding study) looks like laziness from the outside. From the inside, it’s a way of escaping an uncomfortable feeling. Lecturing a teenager about working harder targets the behaviour, but the engine is the thought. If “I’ll fail” changes to “I can finish one chapter tonight,” the feeling and the behaviour usually follow.

## What quietly makes it worse

Most parents are trying their best, and still some common habits raise the temperature:

1. **Comparison.** “Sharma ji’s son has already finished two revisions.” Comparison rarely motivates; it usually confirms the student’s worst fear — that they are not enough.
2. **Talking only about marks.** If every conversation at dinner is about the syllabus, home stops being a place to rest.
3. **Catastrophising out loud.** “If you don’t get 90%, you won’t get anywhere.” Teenagers believe us more than we think.
4. **Taking away all breaks.** Cutting sport, friends and hobbies entirely in exam year removes the very things that help a brain recover.
5. **Last-minute rescue plans.** A new tuition, a new app and a new timetable in the final month can add chaos rather than calm.

## What helps

### Ask about the plan, not the result

Swap “How much will you score?” for “What’s the plan for this week?” Plans are within your child’s control. Results are not. A good weekly plan is specific and modest: which chapters, which days, how much revision, and when the breaks are.

### Make the week visible

Sit with your teenager — once — and put the week on paper. Study blocks of 45–60 minutes with short breaks work for most students. Mark the non-negotiables: sleep, meals, one activity they enjoy. A plan they helped write is one they’re far more likely to follow.

### Protect sleep like it’s part of the syllabus

Memory consolidation happens during sleep. A student who sleeps seven or eight hours will usually retain more than one who stays up until 2 a.m. re-reading notes. Phones out of the bedroom at night helps more than almost anything else.

### Normalise the feeling

Say it plainly: “It makes sense that you feel nervous. This matters to you.” Naming the feeling lowers it. You don’t need to fix it immediately.

### Separate the result from the person

Before results are out, tell your child — in words — that your love and respect don’t depend on a number. It sounds obvious. Many teenagers have never heard it said out loud.

### Watch your own stress

Children read parents’ faces very well. If you’re anxious, it’s worth finding your own outlet — a friend, a walk, a sibling — so the anxiety doesn’t land on your child.

## A 10-minute weekly check-in you can try

Once a week, ideally on a calm evening, try three questions:

1. “What went well this week, even something small?”
2. “What was hard?”
3. “What’s one thing that would make next week a bit easier?”

Then listen. Don’t solve unless asked. If your child picks something to try, offer to help with that one thing only.

## When stress needs more than encouragement

Stress that comes and goes around tests is normal. Please seek professional support if you notice:

- talk of hopelessness, being a burden, or not wanting to be alive;
- not eating or sleeping for days;
- panic attacks, or being unable to go to school;
- self-harm of any kind.

**If you are worried about your child’s immediate safety, call 112.** For free, confidential mental-health support 24×7, call **Tele-MANAS on 14416 or 1-800-891-4416**. A school counsellor or your family doctor can also help you find a qualified professional.

## Where a mentor fits

A mentor isn’t a tutor and doesn’t teach physics. What a mentor offers a board-exam student is a calm, regular conversation with someone outside the family: a place to say “I’m scared I’ll fail” without worrying about disappointing anyone, to untangle the thought behind the avoidance, and to leave each session with one small, realistic next step.
