Between the ages of eight and eleven, children start to form a picture of who they are. “I’m good at drawing.” “I’m bad at maths.” “People don’t like me.” “I can do hard things.” These early beliefs can stay with them for a long time.

Confidence at this age isn’t about being loud or always winning. It’s a quiet sense that says: *I can try, I can make mistakes, and I’ll be okay.* Here’s how to help that grow.

## Praise effort, not just results

“You’re so smart!” feels like a kind thing to say. But children who are praised mainly for being clever can become afraid of tasks where they might not look clever. Why risk a hard puzzle if failing might prove you’re not smart after all?

Try praising what the child *did*:

- “You kept trying even when that sum was tricky.”
- “I noticed you practised your lines every evening.”
- “You were brave to ask the teacher a question.”

This kind of praise tells a child that confidence comes from things within their control.

## Let them struggle (a bit)

When a child is stuck on a zip, a puzzle or a homework question, it’s tempting to jump in. But each time they work it out themselves, they collect evidence that they’re capable.

Wait a little longer than feels comfortable before helping. When you do help, offer a hint rather than the answer: “What could you try first?”

## Help them handle losing

Losing a game, a race or a quiz can feel enormous at this age. Some children cry, some get angry, some refuse to play again. These are normal reactions to a big feeling.

What helps:

1. **Name the feeling.** “You’re really disappointed you lost.”
2. **Calm the body first.** A few slow breaths, a glass of water, a short walk.
3. **Then talk.** “What was hard about it? What could you try next time?”
4. **Model good losing yourself.** Lose a board game gracefully, out loud: “Oh, I lost! That’s okay, it was fun. Rematch?”

Children who learn that losing is survivable become much more willing to try new things.

## Give them real responsibilities

Confidence grows from being trusted. Age-appropriate jobs — watering plants, packing their own school bag, helping cook a simple dish, looking after a younger sibling for five minutes — show a child that they’re capable and that their contribution matters.

Choose tasks they can genuinely succeed at, and resist the urge to redo them perfectly afterwards.

## Practise speaking up in safe places

Many children at this age are shy in class. You can help them practise in low-stakes settings:

- let them order their own food at a restaurant;
- ask them to call a grandparent and tell them about their week;
- role-play asking a teacher for help, with you playing the teacher (and being silly, so it feels fun).

Small successes in safe places carry over into harder places.

## Watch the words you use about them

Children absorb labels quickly: “She’s the shy one.” “He’s not a maths person.” “She’s so sensitive.” Even said lovingly, these can become self-fulfilling.

Try describing the behaviour instead: “She takes time to warm up to new people.” That leaves room to grow.

## Help them name their feelings

Children who can say “I feel nervous” or “I feel left out” are better able to manage those feelings — and less likely to express them through tantrums or withdrawal. Feelings charts, stories and drawing are great tools at this age. Ask about characters in books: “How do you think he felt when that happened?”

## Celebrate small promises kept

Keeping a small promise — to a friend, to a parent, or to themselves — is a powerful confidence builder. Help your child make tiny, specific commitments (“I’ll read for 10 minutes before bed this week”) and notice when they keep them. Over time, they learn: *I am someone who does what I say.*

## When to look for more support

Shyness and self-doubt are normal. Consider speaking to your child’s teacher, a school counsellor or your doctor if your child is very anxious about going to school, has lost interest in things they used to enjoy, or seems persistently sad. **For free 24×7 mental-health guidance, call Tele-MANAS on 14416 or 1-800-891-4416.** In an emergency, call 112.
